*The Story of Southern Islet* depicts roughly three paths open to Malaysian Chinese in the 1980s, two of which the film portrays fairly clearly. The first was to insist on a Chinese education for one’s children, sending them to Chinese-language schools, while holding on to Chinese—more precisely, in many cases Hokkien—folk beliefs. The second was to change one’s cultural identity and actively integrate into the mainstream culture: changing the children’s surnames, using Malay in everyday life, converting to Islam, and taking up the local folk customs shown in the film.

The third path was to (keep) running away, wanting neither the Malay Muslims nor Greater China. Many first-generation “exam grinders” left for Britain, Australia, Canada, and the United States in the 1980s. Their children’s lives and finances improved a great deal; they went on to elite universities and became doctors, accountants, and lawyers. But the rise in education and income, added up across individuals, did not solve the political marginalization and systematic exclusion by other ethnic groups that this group faces wherever it goes.

By the second generation of exam grinders, the third path had more or less come back to the choices of the first two, except that the first path had become Confucius Institutes, transnational cooperation and exchange programs, and the like, and the second path was no longer the crescent moon paired with Malay but English paired with the cross. Whichever path one takes, breaking out of the cycle of the generations before will take several more generations of feeling one’s way.
