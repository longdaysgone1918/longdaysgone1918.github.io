1.

The Lord of the Rings series (the trilogy, *The Hobbit*, *The Silmarillion*) (J. R. R. Tolkien)

That was when the Lord of the Rings and Harry Potter films and novels were at the height of their popularity. Two classmates lent me *The Two Towers* and *The Goblet of Fire*. I picked the former purely because the Ranger of Ithilien on the cover of the paperback edition looked so cool; I picked the latter because that classmate had brought Harry Potter books one to four to lend out, and I chose the thickest one. I read it over a weekend, forgetting to eat and sleep, to the point that I rushed through my homework on Monday morning. The former, by contrast, took me a whole semester plus a summer vacation, until I was forced to give it back at the start of sixth grade.

Back then, Yilin Press’s paperback edition of The Lord of the Rings was not hard to find in bookstores, and a year later I bought the same edition myself. It was not the one translated by Zhu Shenghao. There were four volumes, printed on pale green paper. The oil paintings on the covers of the trilogy showed, in turn: Gandalf on the stone bridge of Khazad-dûm, holding back the Balrog, Morgoth’s legacy; Sam and Frodo, passing through the forests of Ithilien, being discovered by Faramir and the other Rangers of Gondor; and Théoden, King of Rohan, at dawn during the siege of Minas Tirith, exchanging intelligence about the siege with the natives of the forest not far from the city. The cover of *The Hobbit* reused the oil painting of the nine members of the Fellowship from the first volume, *The Fellowship of the Ring*, like a family portrait. Yilin also had a full-color companion book, which later disappeared and which I never managed to buy. I think it was called *Mojie Meiying* (《魔戒魅影》): it presented the content of the first film from 2001 as an illustrated color encyclopedia, with entries for races, characters, and so on, priced at 19 yuan. At home we had a little basket shaped like a shar-pei, in which I had specially saved up more than twenty coins to buy that book. A few years ago, when I went home, I still saw it by the glass in the entryway; I don’t know if it is still there now. A couple of years later I also got *The Silmarillion*, but what impressed me most was the side story in the appendix, “The Downfall of Númenor,” and I very much hoped it would be brought to the screen. But later I learned that Amazon had made rings of power. Maybe it is my childhood filter, but I found the series dull, crammed with multiple storylines that made little sense.

The covers mentioned above come from <http://group.mtime.com/humansimtolkien/discussion/595020/>

During my middle school years, reading these novels again and again gave me an enormous interest in long chronicles, epic plots, dramatic characters, all kinds of races, and grandly constructed fantasy worlds. Because of this, I did not read much of the more widely acknowledged classics of world literature. In the spring of the year I took the gaokao, when time was already very tight, the classmate sitting in front of me lent me a DVD of the director’s cut of the third Lord of the Rings film. I found a weekend afternoon and watched all four-plus hours of it alone at home, and so a journey of nearly ten years came to this fine ending.

2.

*Witnessed* (Budd Hopkins) and *The Idiot* (Dostoevsky)

I bought these two books earlier than the Lord of the Rings series, but it was many years before I felt like reading them. They were by-products of a buy-one-get-one-free sale at a supermarket bookstore, ten yuan. Thinking of how deep their influence turned out to be, I cannot help wondering whether fate is constant or capricious. And supermarket bookstores now seem to be aimed at younger and younger readers; I have not seen those relatively serious books in supermarkets since. Or rather, because supermarket business models have changed in recent years, paper books are now rarely seen in supermarkets at all.

But I digress. At that time, my mom and I went to the supermarket to shop and stopped by the discount book section. Out of curiosity about the title, and because of the discount, I bought Dostoevsky’s *The Idiot*. (This was much like what happened later when I read *One Hundred Years of Solitude*: it was described as Latin American magical realism, and I expected something like Borges’s “The Garden of Forking Paths.” All I can say is that some books only mean something once you reach a certain age.) That copy of *The Idiot* is now on the table in the entryway; my mom probably has not read it in a long time. The other book was Budd Hopkins’s *Witnessed*. I never quite understood why, out of so many optional add-on books back then, I chose this one. Was it the only copy left on the shelf? Did the big eyes on the cover draw me in? Or was it something else, or all of these? In any case, for about seven years after I bought it, this book and *The Idiot* just sat gathering dust, and I had no desire at all to read them.

Later, during one winter vacation at home, I was so bored that I went looking for something to read to pass the time. I first finished *Spycatcher*, the MI5 memoir published by Jilin Literature and History Press in 1988, and then read *Witnessed*. This book would probably make a brilliant crime series: it concerns a mysterious incident involving the United Nations and the area under the Brooklyn Bridge at the end of the 1980s, near the end of the Cold War. Netflix did later release a documentary about it. I could sense the guiding hand of the narrative behind it, but seeing the people involved on camera still gave me a feeling of detachment. I was once very interested in fireballs, and I asked a researcher at UCLA many questions about them.

Back to *The Idiot*. Unlike *The Brothers Karamazov* or *Crime and Punishment*, it is not usually in the most prominent position among Dostoevsky’s works on bookstore shelves; I have not yet had a chance to finish the latter two. Dostoevsky’s descriptions of detail are striking. I feel there is even a kind of sadness in these details, as if he especially wanted everyone to remember them. The book has many characters, but some of its adult plotlines gave me a slightly sad sense of reality, because I know that some people really are this depraved, and that such people still come to a good end. Others, the victims, the innocent ones, such as Prince Myshkin, miss the chance to bring happiness to themselves and to others precisely because of their sincerity or sense of justice, which is also a pity. It took me a long time to finish this book, so long that by the time I finished it, my life experience was already very different from when I had started. Later I found a review by Herman Hesse that I thought was very well said, and perhaps because of it, this book and *Crime and Punishment* came to complement each other in terms of values. That review is included in the Reviews section.

3.

*Technopoly* (Neil Postman)

One morning, I was sitting in a “black spot” (a seat not in the library’s seat-booking system) on the basement floor of the library, working on an English-language assignment. I can no longer remember what that assignment was about, but I still remember that I had no great liking for that teacher. I did not dislike her either; it was just that she taught the course in a rather lifeless way. You teachers serve as independent directors for all sorts of companies outside, so you could perfectly well combine the theory of this course with students’ practice outside class. At that moment I put down my pen, turned around, and pulled a blue-covered book from among a row of light-colored books on the bottom shelf behind me. I flipped casually through the table of contents; it seemed to be something about journalism and communication. I was especially drawn to the content of Chapter 8, “Invisible Technologies,” which offers a systematic, theoretical analysis of the origins of the abuse of quantitative evaluation techniques. Reading it, while struggling with my assignment, was immensely satisfying.

Later, inspired by it, I began tinkering on my own for a while; I will skip the complicated process and the various things like investigations, lost contacts, collaborations, deaths, and matters that went nowhere. That is how I came to know of Macve and Chandler, since the author cites their respective arguments in Chapter 8 of this book. One day a friend showed me the book *Disciplines, Knowledge, Power* (《学科、知识、权力》); one of its essays was by the author mentioned above, and its content was very close to what he had advocated before. From then on I was influenced by his views and began to pay attention to Foucauldianism. Chandler, meanwhile, had died more than ten years earlier, and the traditional school they represented had long since been buried by newer quantitative methods. When I went to Lowell in person a couple of years ago, I only felt I had come too late: most of what I had seen in books had disappeared. The streets were full of lease and storage ads, and a group of idle teenagers were playing in the desolate area around what had once been the chimney of the BMC warehouse.

Starting from stumbling upon this book by chance, it opened up what was probably the most interesting and meaningful thing I did in those years, and its influence carries on to this day. Before I later left school, I went more than once to look for this book near the black spot, which was later demolished, and never found it. Unwilling to give up, I searched for it many times on the catalog terminals: either it was checked out, or it was listed as in the library but was not there when I went. In any case, I never once found it on the shelf again after that. I had no choice but to look for it online and recommend it to others. After graduating I bought two copies, one to keep and one to give away, cherishing this hard-won coincidence.

4.

*Band of Brothers* (Ambrose)

The translation of this book was brought into mainland China almost at the same time as the HBO series; of course, the book was published just ahead of the series airing on CCTV. My classmate Lin bought the book, and I, always one to take a little advantage, borrowed it and never gave it back. Only eleven years later did I buy a copy of the Yilin collector’s edition to make it up to him. He said he had never actually read it back then.

I originally thought this book would tell stories of the paratroopers’ fierce battles from Normandy to the end of the war, given its cover and the series of black-and-white photographs at the beginning. But in fact Ambrose wrote more about people: the daily lives, thoughts, and later recollections of ordinary people against the vast backdrop of the war, before they became witnesses of their time, occasionally intertwined with famous historical figures, and so on. The fierce battle scenes I had imagined, and the vivid personalities answering each other as in a film or TV series, did not appear here. Still, it drew me to read it many times. The book came with a copy of the topographic map of the Normandy area that each of the paratroopers carried back then, which felt a bit like looking back on major historical events together with these ordinary people: from the beaches to inland, from one town to another, from the bridges of the Netherlands to the forests of the Ardennes… Before and after enlisting, they had been students, drivers, cooks, glassworks workers, writers, fertilizer plant workers, lawyers, from all walks of life. This gave me a subtle sense of closeness, as if feeling the world without having to worry about being hurt by it.

Later, as CCTV brought in the series and aired it many times, what impressed me most was the first broadcast, which I think was around six or seven in the evening on CCTV-8. It was the original, before CCTV later cut it according to its own judgment. Even though I had already read the book, I was still deeply drawn in by the scenes, the music, and the way the story was told. It gave a feeling that was grand, open, slightly melancholy, and full yet impossible to recapture. One could say this feeling filled in beautifully the emotional calm that comes from reading the text, and was even more evocative.

Tracks 1/2/3/19/20 of the OST are especially moving; I often take them out and listen to them.

This book gave me some interesting thoughts and ideas, including a kind of reverie about everyday life in an era I never lived through, and a sense of helplessness about, and tenderness toward, people, things, and memories that are lost and cannot be regained, and that will in the end inevitably disappear. And, of course, friendship, impulsiveness, endurance, trust, and a kind of sympathy for the vast numbers of ordinary participants on every side of that world war, now more than half a century past. This feeling is much like the last episode of the 1973 BBC documentary about the Second World War, which is about memory. Probably because the people of that era have mostly passed away, the center of the narrative has shifted from commemorating a great past era to guarding against the enemy within today. I feel that in whatever country it may be, films and TV series about urban ex-soldier kings, special police, or martial-arts masters have become a kind of safe bet aimed at particular audiences. The plots do not need much historical depth, and the storylines are all much the same and do not bear much scrutiny; the focus is on having the hero play the fool to beat his enemies, and finally return to his family.

What is past cannot be remonstrated with; what is to come can still be pursued.

5.

Bullshit Jobs (David Graeber)

When this book was published, it was still the era of Occupy Wall Street. The kind of so-called intermediate jobs the book points to, which neither directly create products nor directly touch production materials or sources, have indeed been hit quite hard now. Back then hardly anyone was discussing how AI would hit the real economy; instead, in reviews one would see from time to time people agreeing that such jobs had no value. Now such voices have been completely drowned out by the economic downturn and fear of AI, to the point of being invisible.

I have never liked how everything is summed up as AI these days. The word AI is far too broad: from some science fiction of the 20th century to the technological progress of every decade after 2000, you can see that what people discussed as AI in different periods was not the same thing at all. Even now, when people bring up AI, it is more a kind of projection of the fear of technology replacing human labor. Interestingly, at the beginning of this year, I unexpectedly saw this book again, after a long time, in a local bookstore, placed next to *the dawn of everything*, which he had not yet finished before he died. I could not help feeling gratified.

6.

The Baron in the Trees (Calvino)

This book is very thin; if you read fast, you could probably finish it in a day or two. I feel the protagonist is like a sunny version of Prince Myshkin; or rather, if Myshkin had lived in Italy, he would probably look like the hero of this book. I can no longer remember many of the specific plot points, but it must have discussed topics such as love and free will, because all I remember is that I enjoyed reading it immensely at the time. I am grateful that my high school textbook included Borges’s “The Garden of Forking Paths,” which added a touch of color to our dull lives back then.

7.

Albion’s Seed (David Hackett Fischer)

Here I will just post the link to the review; it was because I saw this bilingual review back then that I went to read the book. The link to the original English review can also be found there. If strong fundraising capacity and military-technological strength are the face America presents to the world, then this book, about how several different waves of immigration to the New England region formed the cornerstone of American culture, is instead the other, inner face. Although I found this book during the end  of Obama’s 2nd term, it remains timely enough to explain some of the reasons for today’s fractured social consensus. It is very important for individuals to see clearly where they really stand in the tide of society, but admitting this truly takes courage.
